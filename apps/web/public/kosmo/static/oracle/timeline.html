From the Abacus to the Singularity

Four thousand years of computation, seventy of AI, and the measured growth rates that current timeline claims rest on

Timeline claims are usually stated as single dates. Almost none of them are derived from a benchmark. This page separates three things that get merged: what has been measured, what has been extrapolated from it, and what has merely been asserted. The chronology is the part with evidence behind it. Everything after 2026 is arithmetic on a curve, and the curve is already meeting physical limits.

The short answer. Computation has improved at a staggeringly steady rate for eighty-six years — compute per dollar has doubled roughly every 2.3 years since 1940, a factor of about 1011. AI capability has followed a faster curve for six years. But AGI, superintelligence and the Singularity are three different kinds of claim, and only the first is the kind a benchmark can address. Current forecasts for "human-level AI" span 2027 to 2116, and the spread reflects definitional disagreement more than technological uncertainty.

The chronology

Four threads run through it, colour-coded below. The substrate is the hardware and architecture. Capability is what the software can do. Life span is the other clock the machine is racing: how long people live, and whether the extra years arrive in a state worth having. Theory is what people claimed the machine would become. Five of the forty-four entries are theory, and every one of them was written before the thing it described existed.

The life-span track was not here in earlier versions of this page, which is a real omission rather than a presentational one. The labour force in 2060 is decided by two things: how many people are alive and how many of them are well enough to work. A ten-year gain in working life is a decade of extra output; a ten-year gain in dependency costs the same economy twice. Neither shows up in any compute benchmark, and both change the answer.

On the numbers. Each entry is tagged verified, standard or flagged. Nothing before 1940 has a meaningful operations-per-second figure — tally notches, abacus beads, gear ratios and FLOPS are not commensurable, and any long-run cost series before the electronic era is an illustration rather than a measurement. The 1946 and 1971 figures are real and documented; the 1837 figure is Babbage's own estimate for a machine that was never built.

Where the curve goes

Training compute for the largest known model, on a log scale. The dashed line is the measured rate since 2020 — 5×/yr, a doubling every 5.2 months. The solid line is your choice. The red band at the top is the latency wall: above roughly 1030 FLOP, a single training run cannot synchronise across a cluster fast enough, whatever the chips.

The thresholds are real policy and physics, not invented for the chart. California's SB 53 defines a frontier model as one trained above 1026 FLOP — GPT-4.5 crossed it. Epoch AI estimates a 2×1029 FLOP run is likely feasible by 2030. The latency ceiling of 3×1030 to 1032 FLOP is their hard cap absent new interconnect topologies. One caveat on the recent points: GPT-5 is estimated at around 5×1025 FLOP — less than GPT-4.5 — because effort shifted to post-training and inference-time compute. The frontier is no longer simply the biggest training run, which is the first sign that this particular curve is losing its grip.

The measured rates

These are the benchmarks of growth that timeline claims are built from. They are not the same rate, they do not compound into one another, and combining them is the most common error in the genre. Note especially that the long-run rate is slower than Moore's law — because Moore's law is transistors, not compute per dollar, and the two diverged decades ago.

The one number that matters most here. Compute per dollar has doubled every 2.32 years since 1940 — about 0.13 orders of magnitude a year, 10× every 7.7 years, a factor of roughly 1011 in total. Different studies put the doubling anywhere from 1.5 years (Median Group) to 4.4 years (Bergal), which over eighty-six years is a spread of nine orders of magnitude. Anyone quoting a single compute trend is quoting one study's fit.

The five walls

Extrapolation is only valid until something binds. Five things are visibly binding now, and they are ranked by how soon. The disturbing part is not the list; it is that the two firmest walls — power and latency — are physical, while the one most often invoked as the rescue — algorithmic progress — is the weakest measured rate here and contributes the minority of observed gains.

The forecast spread

Every dated prediction for AGI, superintelligence or transformative AI that I could source, plotted on one axis. Position is the predicted year; colour is the kind of evidence behind it. The point of the chart is the spread — 2027 to 2116 for what people loosely call the same event.

Two clues about how soft these numbers are, both internal to the surveys themselves. The same 2023 expert survey returns 2047 for "all tasks" and 2116 for "all occupations" — arguably the same question, answered seventy years apart. And the same survey instrument moved thirteen years in one year between its 2022 and 2023 editions. That is a change in the news, not in the technology.

AGI vs superintelligence vs the Singularity

These are routinely used as if they were one target at different distances. They are three different kinds of claim, and the difference determines whether a benchmark could in principle settle it.

The practical consequence. AGI is a coverage claim — which tasks. Superintelligence is a degree claim — how much better than the best humans, across nearly everything. The Singularity is a predictive-discontinuity claim — that forecasting stops working. You can have AGI with a slow takeoff, superintelligence without discontinuity, and an intelligence explosion that is superhuman but not singular. Most public argument about "when AGI" is really about the third thing while citing the first.

The track record, and the winters

Forecasting this field has a history worth knowing before trusting any date on this page — including the ones I have just plotted.

What the winters actually punished. Neither collapse came from the core technology failing. 1973 punished an over-claim about scope — reasoning would generalise from toy problems. 1987 punished a hardware bet — specialised Lisp machines lost to general-purpose workstations. Both are live risks now: the first in the assumption that benchmark scores transfer to messy contexts, the second in the assumption that purpose-built accelerators stay ahead of general-purpose ones.

How to read the dates

A closing note on method, because the timeline above is only as good as how it is used.

Separate the measurement from the extrapolation

That training compute has grown 5×/yr since 2020 is a measurement with a tight confidence interval. That it will continue is an assumption. The page keeps these visually separate — dotted for measured, solid for assumed — and so should any argument built on them.

Do not compound the rates

Training compute, chip efficiency, algorithmic efficiency and inference price are four different quantities with four different sources and four different confidence levels. Multiplying them is a common move and it is not legitimate: the algorithmic share of measured progress has been declining, and the fastest price declines come from a mid-tier that is not the frontier.

Ask what the date is a date for

"AGI by 2033" can mean a coverage threshold, a degree threshold, or a scenario's pacing. The single most useful question for any timeline claim is which of the three it means, and almost no popular claim answers it.

Watch the instruments, not the models

MMLU, GSM8K and MATH are saturated; GPQA is compressed to noise at the top; METR's task suite cannot resolve above sixteen hours. Capability is still rising while the instruments that measured it stop discriminating — which means the next few years of "AI progress" will be unusually hard to assess, and unusually easy to over-claim in either direction.

What would change this page. A demonstrated substitute for human text at scale; a working non-CMOS substrate that resets the efficiency curve; a frontier model that again requires more compute than its predecessor rather than less; or three consecutive years where the task-horizon curve flattens on a suite that can still resolve the frontier. Any one of those would move the central estimates by a decade.
