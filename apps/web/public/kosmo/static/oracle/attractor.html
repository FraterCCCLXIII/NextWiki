Three Words for the Same Predicament

Polycrisis, metacrisis, third attractor — what each one claims, who made it, and where the evidence thins

Two bodies of material sit on this page and are kept apart. The corpus is the public output of a 72-conversation project: 1,210 codings, fifteen propositions, twenty paradoxes. The literature is where the three terms came from, with primary sources. The corpus is evidence about what a community thinks. The literature is evidence about what the words mean. Neither settles the other.

What this page is not. It is not an endorsement of the metacrisis framing, nor a debunking. It is an attempt to separate three things that routinely get merged: the description of the crisis, the claim about its cause, and the claim about where to go. Each rests on different evidence, and only the first one has much of it.

The three terms

They are often used interchangeably. They are not interchangeable, and the differences are the whole argument.

The one-line version

Polycrisis says the crises are entangled. Metacrisis says they have a common cause. Third attractor says there is somewhere else to go. The first is a description, the second is a diagnosis, the third is a prescription — and the evidence gets thinner at each step.

Provenance, 1970–2026

Both terms have older roots than their current fame suggests, and the order in which they became famous is the reverse of the order in which they were made. Metacrisis has priority in the literature — 1970, 2011, 2015 — but polycrisis became the public word first in 2022. Much of the current framing of metacrisis as a correction to polycrisis is retrofitting.

    Dating caution: the French polycrise appears in Terre-Patrie (1993) and in English in 1999. The Polycrisis Observatory itself notes that 1993 is a bibliographic marker, not proof of first occurrence. Sources regularly state 1999, and at least one places the coinage in “the 1970s”; both are wrong.

    The three attractors

    Not three policies. Three basins — states the system falls into and stays in. The argument that makes this more than a metaphor is that the first two feed each other: every move away from chaos strengthens control, and every move away from control strengthens chaos.

    Attribution caution: Schmachtenberger developed this across podcasts and films, and there is no single canonical text where all three attractors are defined in numbered form. The clearest published summary is Benjamin Life’s, quoted on the P2P Foundation wiki; the clearest naming of the two failure attractors in writing is from the Your Undivided Attention episode. Popular retellings frequently renumber them — some render attractor 1 as “tribalism” and attractor 2 as “the current industrial model”, which is not his frame.

    Why the middle is hard

    A schematic of the argument, not a model of the world. The horizontal axis is the coordination regime; the vertical is how strongly the system is pulled toward each state. Two failure basins sit at the walls. The third sits between them and is shallower by default, which is the whole problem.

    Move the slider and the middle basin deepens. That is the entire content of the claim: the third attractor is not achieved by preferring it, but by building anti-rivalrous structure — arrangements in which one actor’s gain is not another’s loss — until the middle is a place the system can rest rather than a ridge it rolls off. The shape is invented for legibility; the parameters mean nothing.

    The generators

    Schmachtenberger’s written account gives two generator functions with a third underneath them, not the flat list of three that interview summaries usually report. Both versions are real; they are different granulations of one argument.

    The corpus

    Co-creating the Third Attractor is a podcast and research project by Daniela Bomatter. One question is prepared for each guest, the conversation is allowed to go where it goes, and afterwards the transcripts are read against a fixed set of propositions. This is the most systematic attempt I have seen to turn a podcast into a dataset — and it is worth being precise about what kind of dataset it is.

    Twenty paradoxes

    The corpus’s own synthesis, published before the claims and the thing the claims were built from. Each pole is true; each pole becomes its own worst version when held alone. Bomatter calls them paradoxes rather than polarities deliberately — a polarity gets balanced, a paradox gets held, and holding is a trainable capacity rather than a decision.

    Quantitative anchors

    This literature is mostly qualitative, and the numbers that circulate around it come from adjacent fields rather than from its own authors. Each is tagged by how much weight it will bear.

    The honest summary. Ord’s “1 in 6” is one philosopher’s synthesis and he says so. The p(doom) spread — from below 0.01% to above 99% among people who study this for a living — is a more important fact than any central estimate. The planetary boundary and nuclear figures are measured and contested at the margin. The topsoil numbers in circulation are inflated by a factor of two. Do not add these together; they are in different units and different centuries.

    The criticisms

    Strongest first. The most damaging objections come from inside the framing — people who accept the predicament and reject the label or the prescription.

    What is missing from the critical literature. There is no published scholarly critique of the third attractor arguing that it is unfalsifiable or that it lacks a theory of transition, even though both are the obvious objections. The nearest documented version is McLeod’s “politically implausible”. That absence is itself a finding: the framing has been discussed almost entirely by people sympathetic to it.
