Abundance

Jobs, healthcare, savings, housing and a basic income in an economy that needs few workers

Levers

Measured 2026 anchors are fixed; everything you can move is a policy choice or an assumption about behaviour. The model is accounting, not forecasting — it says whether a bundle balances, not whether it will be chosen.

The claim being tested. Every guarantee in the ask is a claim on output: a job is a wage, healthcare is a service, housing is a building, savings is deferred income, a basic income is a transfer. None of them can be produced by willing them. This page prices each one, funds it from a tax base that is shrinking, and shows what happens to the arithmetic as the wage bill falls from 58% of GDP to 27%.

How the thing actually works

1. The guarantee is a claim on output

A basic income of $900 a month for every adult is 9.4% of GDP, and it buys nothing unless the economy produces goods someone can buy with it. Housing is 1% of GDP in construction but the stock is three times GDP. Health is 16.6% of GDP and rising. A job guarantee paying three-quarters of the average wage costs $80,000 a year per participant, which is why the ceiling matters more than the promise.

The model prices all five and adds them. That total is the number that has to be funded, and it is between 18 and 30 percent of GDP depending on the settings.

2. The tax base is the wage bill

Income tax on labour is 32% of a wage bill that is 58% of GDP today. When labour's share falls to 27%, that base halves even if the rate does not move. So a state that funds its guarantees from wages is running into a wall that is already scheduled.

Three bases survive automation: consumption, which is still 60% of GDP; land, which cannot be offshored; and capital income, which grows exactly as labour's share falls. The chart on this page is mostly the story of revenue migrating between those four.

3. Ownership is the hinge

A sovereign fund that owns a share of the capital stock earns a return whether or not anyone works. The model caps its share of the capital stock at 50% and the annual contribution at 3.5% of GDP — both far beyond anything attempted, and both necessary for the arithmetic to close.

Once the fund is large enough, the basic income is paid out of capital income and the tax cost falls to zero. That is the transition: not a tax that rises for ever, but a tax that buys assets and then stops needing to.

The transition, practically

Build the meter before the bill

Nothing in this is administratively new. Every rich country already runs a retirement pension, a health insurer, a housing programme and a tax authority. The scarce input is sequencing: the fund has to start buying capital while the wage base is still large, because the capital tax that seeds it is levied on a wage economy.

The sensitivity bars at the bottom test what actually moves the transition, and the answer is uncomfortable: the depth of the dip is set mostly by how much you promise, not by how fast the machines arrive. A generous payment made before the fund matures is a payment you cannot keep.

Pay for care, not for idleness

The job guarantee is not make-work in this model: 45–60% of the wage is counted as real output, because care, repair, restoration and community work are services people want and markets under-provide. That is also the only large sector where productivity resists automation, so it is where the remaining jobs are.

But a guarantee that pays three-quarters of the average wage for a quarter of the output is expensive, and the model's ceiling is a budget constraint rather than a moral one. At 22% of adults it is the single largest line item.

The three ways it breaks

Demand: if the transfers arrive without the fund, the state borrows, the debt compounds, the closure rule cuts the payment, and the floor falls exactly when it is needed. Inflation: if the payment is financed by creation rather than taxation, the floor is denominated in money that buys less each year. Sequencing: if the fund is delayed past the wage cliff, the assets are bought at the top with a tax base that has already gone.

What this cannot tell you

The political economy is absent

Nothing here models whether a 45% tax take is electable, whether capital is mobile, or whether a state that owns half the capital stock remains a democracy. Those are the binding constraints in practice, and they are unmodelled.

Prices are doing too much work

Housing is modelled as units and construction cost, not as a market with land prices; health as a share of GDP that drifts, not as a supply of clinicians. The physical ledgers are the weakest part and they are the part a real programme would fail on first.

It is one country

A closed economy. No trade, no migration, no capital flight, no reserve currency. For the United States those omissions are large and they all point the same way: the real version is harder than this one.
