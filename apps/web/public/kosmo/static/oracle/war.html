War

Every other page here assumes the future is an economy. This one is the reason that assumption has never held for long — and why a $500 aircraft just changed what a war costs

Controls

The year control moves the country frame on the war-versus-income chart. It does not filter the long series, which are world totals: eight frames of country data is all the panel supports once income has to be joined in. Play steps those eight frames, and the movement between them is a transition drawn over 0.8s — nothing is interpolated in the numbers, and the year never shows a frame that does not exist.

What these numbers are, and what they leave out. Everything left of the last measured year is UCDP's conflict register, UNHCR's displacement caseload and the World Bank's military spending. Nothing on this page is modelled, and nothing forecasts. That is deliberate: war is the one series in this lab where a smooth extrapolation would be worse than no line at all. Battle deaths are also the narrowest possible measure of a war's cost — they exclude every indirect death from famine, destroyed hospitals and disease, which in most wars are the larger number.

Drones, and the collapse in the price of a strike

Everything above is measured history, and its shape is mostly a story about states fighting each other less. The chart below is the reason that may not continue. It is the one place in this lab where a technology curve and a violence curve meet: the cost of putting one warhead on one target has fallen by four orders of magnitude in thirty years, and the cheapest airframe on the chart is now the primary anti-armour weapon in a European land war.
