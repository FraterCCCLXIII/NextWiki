What is modelled, and what cannot be

The three layers

Energy. Each fuel has a levelised cost. Fossil costs rise with cumulative extraction against ultimately recoverable resources, and with whatever carbon price actually reaches them. Clean costs fall on a learning curve but rise with penetration, because storage and grid are real. Share moves toward the cheapest mix at each fuel's own turnover speed.

Carbon. Emissions enter a four-box impulse response. About a quarter stays airborne essentially forever, which is why the concentration charts do not come back down even when emissions hit zero.

Climate. A two-layer energy balance: a thin mixed layer that responds in years, a deep ocean that takes centuries. That split is what produces committed warming, and it is why 2500 looks nothing like 2100.

Why the price is not enough

Two levers exist because one is not sufficient. Coverage is the share of emissions a price actually reaches — about 24% today, most of it under $30/t. Phase-out is the mandate. In a model where fuels compete on cost, a high price makes coal small but never zero, because some use will always pay it. Every published net-zero pathway closes that gap with regulation, not with a bigger number.

What it cannot tell you

There are no tipping points beyond the one feedback lever, no ice-sheet dynamics, no regional climate, no agriculture or water, no migration, no conflict, and no finance. Population is exogenous. The damage function is a quadratic guess, and the literature disagrees about it by an order of magnitude. Treat the far right of any chart as a shape, not a level.
