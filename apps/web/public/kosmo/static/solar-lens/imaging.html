Target: RA ≈23h 06m 30s, Dec ≈−05° 02′ (approximate J2000 host position). Receiver lies in the opposite direction: RA ≈11h 06m 30s, Dec ≈+05° 02′, here at 650 AU.

What the model does
For every scan point, each source pixel contributes to two opposite ring arcs, with Gaussian angular spread (weights below 0.001 of the peak are omitted for sparse computation) and softened inverse-distance weighting. The same nonnegative forward operator generates photon-noisy measurements and drives a Richardson–Lucy multiplicative inversion. The only spatial prior is a known circular disk. The numerical inverse operates on the same grid as the synthetic measurements; unresolved subpixel texture is averaged before measurement. The continuous input is a visualization reference, not independently validated resolution evidence. All three synthetic color channels are processed independently. Scans cover the selected raster in the projected image plane; partial coverage takes the first rows.

What this does not predict
This is an educational ring-mixing model, not the full solar gravitational lens wave-optical PSF or a mission sensitivity forecast. It includes approximate angular diffraction and detector sampling, quantum efficiency, aperture/exposure scaling, dark shot noise and one read per ring bin per scan. It omits solar-corona photon noise, host-star leakage, orbital motion, rotation, changing clouds, full detector response and navigation errors. Instrument presets are generic assumptions, not product specifications. The Poisson-only inversion is approximate when read noise matters. Reference photon flux is user-assumed, not derived from TRAPPIST-1e brightness. Signal scales as aperture squared × exposure × QE relative to that reference. Three channels are illustrative pseudo-color at one representative wavelength, not three calibrated bandpasses. No absolute SNR or mission feasibility is established. Ring thickness is exaggerated; radial structure is not modeled. The selected reconstruction grid is a computational choice, not a predicted physical resolution.

Geometry at 650 AU
Using a rounded 40-light-year distance and assumed 0.92-Earth-radius planet, the projected planet disk is approximately 3 km wide, from diameter × receiver distance ÷ target distance. This is a length in the receiver's image plane, not telescope sensor size. The scan raster spans that disk. Exact mission targeting requires a propagated ephemeris and tracking the planet's motion.
