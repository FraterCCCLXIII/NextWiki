## Explanation

People often use "mass" and "weight" as if they mean the same thing. In physics they do not.

**Mass** is the amount of matter in an object. It is measured in kilograms (kg). Your mass is the same on Earth, on the Moon and in deep space. It does not depend on where you are.

**Weight** is the force of gravity pulling on an object. A force is measured in newtons (N). Weight changes when gravity changes.

The link between them is a simple rule:

W = m × g

Here W is weight in newtons, m is mass in kilograms, and g is the strength of gravity in metres per second squared (m/s²). The number g is the surface gravity of a world. It tells you how strongly that world pulls on each kilogram.

These values are approximate and rounded:

- Earth: about 9.8 m/s²
- Moon: about 1.6 m/s²
- Mars: about 3.7 m/s²
- Jupiter (at the cloud tops): about 24.8 m/s²

Jupiter has no solid surface, so its "surface" means the level of the cloud tops.

There is a common mix-up. Many people say "I weigh 40 kilograms." Kilograms measure mass, not force. A bathroom scale shows kilograms, but it really measures how hard you press down on it. That is a force. The scale then does the sum for Earth's gravity and shows you a mass. Take that same scale to the Moon and it would give the wrong answer, because it is set for Earth.

Remember: on Earth, each kilogram of mass weighs about 9.8 newtons.

## Worked example

A student has a mass of 40 kg. Find the weight on each world using W = m × g.

- Earth: W = 40 × 9.8 = 392 N
- Moon: W = 40 × 1.6 = 64 N
- Mars: W = 40 × 3.7 = 148 N
- Jupiter cloud tops: W = 40 × 24.8 = 992 N

The mass stays 40 kg in every case. Only the weight changes.

Now compare. The Moon weight divided by the Earth weight is 64 ÷ 392, which is about 0.16, or roughly one sixth. Jupiter's pull is about two and a half times Earth's, since 992 ÷ 392 is about 2.5.

These answers are rounded, because the values of g are rounded.

## Activity

You need: paper, a pencil and a calculator. Nothing else.

1. Pick a mass for yourself or a friend. You can use 40 kg, or a mass you know. Use a pretend object if you prefer, such as a 5 kg backpack.
2. Write W = m × g at the top of the page.
3. Make a table with four rows: Earth, Moon, Mars, Jupiter. Fill in g for each world.
4. Work out the weight in newtons for each world. Show the multiplication.
5. Check by estimating: is the Moon answer about one sixth of the Earth answer?
6. Write one sentence about the mix-up between kilograms and newtons. Explain it to someone at home.

Challenge: if the backpack has a weight of 49 N on Earth, what is its mass? (Divide by 9.8.)

## Summary

- Mass is the amount of matter, in kilograms. It is the same everywhere.
- Weight is a force, in newtons. It depends on gravity: W = m × g.
- Approximate surface gravity: Earth 9.8, Moon 1.6, Mars 3.7, Jupiter cloud tops 24.8 m/s².
- A 40 kg student weighs about 392 N on Earth, 64 N on the Moon, 148 N on Mars and 992 N at Jupiter's cloud tops.
- Bathroom scales show kilograms, but they measure a force.
